What you just heard

Sampling a sine wave at $f_s$ cannot tell the difference between a tone at $f$ and a tone at $f - k f_s$ for any whole number $k$: both pass through exactly the same sample points. What comes back out is the one that falls between $-f_s/2$ and $f_s/2$:

$$ f_{\text{apparent}} = \left| f - f_s \operatorname{round}(f / f_s) \right| $$

So the result of sampling too slowly is not a blurred or noisy signal. It is a different signal, and it is perfectly clean — which is why you cannot tell by listening to the output alone that anything went wrong.

Two things are worth trying. First, hold $f$ fixed and pull $f_s$ down past $2f$: the pitch you hear starts to fall while the tone itself has not changed at all. Second, turn on the filter before sampling: the false tone disappears, but so does the real one. There is no setting in which the original comes back, because nothing after the sampler can undo it — which is why an anti-alias filter has to sit before the sampler in a real instrument.

The reconstruction you are hearing also passes through a low-pass filter at $f_s/2$, the same one a real digital-to-analogue converter uses to smooth the steps back into a wave.
