What you just heard

A digital filter is one line of arithmetic repeated on every sample, and the plane above is a picture of that line. Write the filter as a ratio of two polynomials in $z^{-1}$,

$$ H(z) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)} = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}} $$

and the marks are the roots: circles where the numerator vanishes, crosses where the denominator does. Multiplying the factors back out gives the coefficients on the readout, and those coefficients are the difference equation

$$ y[n] = b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1] + b_2 x[n-2] - a_1 y[n-1] - a_2 y[n-2] $$

which is the loop you would write in Python. Nothing else is going on: drag a mark, watch five numbers change, and that is the whole connection between the picture and the code.

The response is a product of distances. On the unit circle, $z = e^{j\omega}$, and

$$ |H(e^{j\omega})| = |g| \cdot \frac{\prod_k |e^{j\omega} - z_k|}{\prod_k |e^{j\omega} - p_k|} $$

— the distance from the point on the circle to each zero, divided by the distance to each pole. That single line explains everything you just did. Walk a zero towards the circle and one factor in the top goes to nothing, so that frequency leaves the sound; put it exactly on the circle and the gain there is exactly zero, which is why the notch readout says minus infinity. Walk a pole towards the circle and a factor in the bottom goes to nothing instead, so that frequency is lifted above everything else. The angle you drag to is the frequency it happens at, because that is what $\omega$ means in $e^{j\omega}$.

The radius is time. Look at the impulse response while you move the pole radius. A pole at radius $r$ contributes a term $r^n$, so the ringing falls by a factor of $e$ every $-1/\ln r$ samples: at $r = 0.9$ that is about ten samples and you barely hear it, at $r = 0.99$ it is a hundred, and at $r = 0.999$ it is a thousand and the noise turns into a pitch. The same number read the other way is the width of the peak — long in time is narrow in frequency, which is last week's trade-off arriving in a new costume. The readout puts the measured width beside the textbook approximation $\Delta\omega \approx 2(1-r)$, and you can watch that approximation stop being any good as you bring the radius down.

Zeros and poles are not symmetric in one respect. Push the zeros outside the circle and nothing dramatic happens: the magnitude changes shape, the phase changes a lot, and the filter keeps working. Push the poles outside and the sound stops, with a message. The reason is worth stating carefully, because it is the point of the region of convergence. A causal system has $H(z)$ converging for $|z| > \max_k |p_k|$, and the frequency response is $H$ evaluated on the unit circle. While every pole is inside, the circle is in that region and the response means something. Once a pole is on or outside it, the circle is not, and the curve the page draws is no longer the frequency response of anything you can run — which is why it turns grey and dashed rather than disappearing. The impulse response is the honest picture in that case: it is growing, and it keeps growing.

$$ \text{causal and stable} \iff \text{ROC} \supset \{|z| = 1\} \iff |p_k| < 1 \text{ for every } k $$

Take the poles away and the filter changes species. With no denominator there is no $y[n-k]$ on the right-hand side, so each output sample is a fixed combination of a few input samples and nothing else. Feed it a single 1 and the output stops dead after three samples — finite impulse response, and the word finite is literal; count the stems. Put the poles back and it never reaches zero, only smaller and smaller. That is the whole of FIR against IIR: not two families of formula to memorise, but whether the loop reads its own past output.

Why the pairs move together. Every coefficient in that difference equation is a real number, because real hardware multiplies real numbers. A single complex root would break that: it takes a root and its conjugate together to give a real quadratic,

$$ (1 - re^{j\theta}z^{-1})(1 - re^{-j\theta}z^{-1}) = 1 - 2r\cos\theta\, z^{-1} + r^2 z^{-2} $$

which is where the $\cos\theta$ on the readout comes from. Drag either member of a pair and the other follows, because releasing one of them would mean coefficients you could not build. At $\theta = 0$ or $\theta = \pi$ the pair collapses onto the real axis and becomes a repeated real root — that is not a glitch at the edge of the slider, it is the same formula with $\cos\theta = \pm 1$.

One honest note about the volume. A pole at $r = 0.99$ has a peak gain of about 100, and at $r = 0.999$ about 1000. Sending that to your speakers unchanged would be unpleasant at best, so everything is divided by the peak gain before you hear it, and the divisor is on the readout. This means what you actually hear as you sharpen a resonance is not the peak getting louder — it is everything else getting quieter. Those are the same filter and differ only by a constant, but they are not the same experience, so the unscaled peak gain is printed in decibels: the number your ears are not being given is on the screen instead.

Where the numbers come from

The coefficients are obtained by multiplying out the factored form — which is to say, by convolving the coefficient lists of the second-order sections, the same operation as last week's demo, used here on polynomials instead of signals. The frequency response is then evaluated straight from those coefficients at fourteen hundred points across $[0, \pi]$.

Both of those are checked against a second route that never expands the polynomial at all: the product of distances written above, computed factor by factor. The two must agree to within rounding at every point, and an automated test says so. A third check goes further out: the coefficients are compared against what SymPy gets when it expands $\prod_k (1 - p_k z^{-1})$ symbolically, without knowing that it is looking at a filter. The sign in the middle term is the thing all this is guarding, because getting it wrong produces a filter that looks perfectly healthy and resonates at the wrong frequency.

The impulse response is not read off any formula. It is produced by running the difference equation on the sequence $1, 0, 0, \ldots$, which is the same function the audio thread runs on your sound, and a test confirms that those two implementations agree sample for sample. The width of the peak is measured off the response by bisection rather than taken from the approximation, which is why the two numbers on that row can disagree.

What keeps this safe. Three separate things, and they are deliberately redundant. The page refuses to start the sound while any pole is at radius 1 or more, and stops it if you drag one out there while it is playing. Whatever does play is scaled so that its loudest frequency comes out at gain 1, never above. And inside the audio thread itself, every single output sample is checked before it leaves: anything not finite, or larger than four, stops the sound and says so. The third one should never fire. It exists because the first two live on the main thread, and the audio thread keeps running when the main thread does not.

Coefficients are not swapped abruptly when you drag; they are interpolated across one 128-sample block, which is under three milliseconds. That is safe for a reason specific to second order: the stable region for $(a_1, a_2)$ is cut out by three linear inequalities, so it is a triangle, and a straight line between two points inside a triangle stays inside. It would not be safe for a higher-order denominator, and that is one of the reasons there is only one pair of poles on this page.
