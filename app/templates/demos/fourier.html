Fourier series: building a wave out of sines

A target wave is approximated by adding up sines. Choose how many to add, and listen to the result. Then change the phase of those same sines and watch what happens to the picture — and to the sound.

What you just heard

Every one of these waves is a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of one frequency $f_0$. Nothing else is needed, and nothing else is allowed:

$$ x(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a_n \cos 2\pi n f_0 t + b_n \sin 2\pi n f_0 t \right) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \sin(2\pi n f_0 t + \varphi_n) $$

Phase is the point of this page. Set the target to a sawtooth, add twenty harmonics, start the sound, and then switch the phase to randomised. The picture stops looking like a sawtooth altogether — it becomes a shape you would not recognise. The bar chart does not move at all, and neither does what you hear. The amplitudes $A_n$ are untouched; only the $\varphi_n$ changed. Your ear is measuring the amplitudes and is remarkably indifferent to the phases, while your eye is looking at the sum, which depends on both. Two signals can look nothing alike and sound the same. This is the one thing on this page that no printed figure can show you.

The corners. With a square or a sawtooth, look at the step. The sum shoots past the target, and the table says by how much. Raise the number of harmonics and read the three columns separately, because they do three different things. The overshoot heads for $8.95\%$ of the step — not for zero. The ripple gets narrower every time, halving as the number of harmonics doubles. And the largest gap anywhere does not shrink at all. Watch the first column on a square wave and then on a sawtooth: the square comes down to $8.95\%$ from above, the sawtooth climbs up to it from below, and neither one keeps going. This is the Gibbs phenomenon, and the usual expectation — that more terms will eventually give a perfect reproduction — is wrong in a specific way. At every fixed instant away from a step the series does converge to the target, and quickly. But there is no number of terms after which the sum is everywhere close to it. The limit of the overshoot is exactly $\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt - 1 \approx 0.1790$, which is $8.95\%$ of the step of $2$.

Which harmonics, and how fast they die away. The square and the triangle both use only odd harmonics, so the bar chart has gaps; the sawtooth uses all of them. What separates the square from the triangle is the rate: $1/n$ against $1/n^2$. That rate is decided by how smooth the target is. A wave with a step needs its high harmonics badly and gives them up slowly; a continuous one gives them up quickly, which is why the triangle looks almost perfect with five terms and the square does not. It is also why only the discontinuous ones overshoot.

What you are actually hearing. A perfect square wave has harmonics all the way up, and your sound card cannot represent the ones above half its sampling rate — they would fold back down to the wrong frequencies, which is a different demo. So the target you can listen to here is itself a partial sum, just with a couple of hundred terms instead of five. The readout tells you how many fit. One more honest detail: the half-wave rectified sine has a constant term $a_0/2 = 1/\pi$, which you can see in the graph but cannot hear — a constant is not a sound.

Where the coefficients come from

The coefficients are not guessed and not fitted. Each one is an integral, and the reason it works is that different harmonics are orthogonal over one period: multiply two of them together, integrate over a period, and you get zero unless they are the same harmonic.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^{T} x(t)\cos 2\pi n f_0 t\,dt, \qquad b_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^{T} x(t)\sin 2\pi n f_0 t\,dt $$

So $b_n$ asks "how much of $\sin 2\pi n f_0 t$ is in $x$?", and the integral answers it because every other harmonic contributes nothing. For the square wave, $x$ is odd, so every $a_n$ is zero; and $x$ has half-wave symmetry, so every even $b_n$ is zero too. What is left is $b_n = 4/(n\pi)$ for odd $n$ — the bars you see, and the $1/n$ envelope drawn over them.

$$ x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{i 2\pi n f_0 t}, \qquad c_n = \frac{a_n - i b_n}{2}, \qquad c_{-n} = \overline{c_n} $$

The exponential form carries exactly the same information written differently. Two things about it are worth keeping: $|c_n| = A_n/2$, because the amplitude is split between $+n$ and $-n$; and $c_{-n}$ is the complex conjugate of $c_n$, which is what makes the sum real. The same conjugate symmetry is why a spectrum of a real signal only needs to be drawn for positive frequencies.

—

About the numbers on this page. The closed forms above are what the page computes with. A set of automated tests checks them against the integrals themselves, evaluated symbolically, and checks the overshoot against its exact value, so the percentages in the table are not measured off the picture.
