What you just saw

The Fourier transform of a pulse, and its inverse, are

$$ X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\,dt, \qquad x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(f)\, e^{+i 2\pi f t}\,df $$

Narrow in time is wide in frequency, and there is no way around it. Halve the width and the whole spectrum stretches by a factor of two, while its height halves — the area under the pulse is what the spectrum reads at $f = 0$, so a shorter pulse also carries less. The table makes the trade quantitative: the first zero sits at $1/T$ for the rectangle and $2/T$ for the other two that stop, and the product $B \cdot T$ in the last column does not move at all as you drag the width. It is a property of the shape, not of the width. A shape has one number here, and choosing a width chooses one point along a hyperbola, never a point off it.

$$ x(at) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{|a|} X\!\left(\frac{f}{a}\right) $$

The Gaussian is its own transform. With the normalisation used here, $x(t) = e^{-\pi t^2/T^2}$ transforms to $X(f) = T e^{-\pi f^2 T^2}$ — a Gaussian again, of width $1/T$. It is the only shape on this page whose two pictures have the same outline, and if the width could be set to exactly one second the two curves would be the identical function of their own variable. The slider does not reach that far, but the statement does not depend on the width: stretching one picture always squeezes the other by the same factor, and the family is closed under the transform. It is also the shape that comes closest to the limit in the next paragraph, and those two facts are the same fact.

There is a floor under the trade-off. Define the spread in each domain the way you would define a standard deviation, $\Delta t^2 = \int t^2 |x|^2 dt / \int |x|^2 dt$ and the same for $\Delta f$ with $|X|^2$. Then for every signal

$$ \Delta t \cdot \Delta f \;\ge\; \frac{1}{4\pi} \approx 0.0796 $$

and the Gaussian attains it exactly. Switch between the shapes and read the row: Gaussian $0.0796$, raised cosine $0.0817$, triangle $0.0872$. The rectangle reports no figure at all, and the reason is worth a moment. Its edges are jumps, so its spectrum only dies away like $1/f$, and $\int f^2 |X|^2 df$ does not converge. Its RMS bandwidth is infinite. That is not a defect of the arithmetic on this page; it is what a perfectly sharp edge costs. Compare the tails of the four shapes on a wide frequency axis and the ordering is the ordering of how smooth they are in time.

A shift moves nothing in the magnitude. Drag the time shift and watch the middle graph: not one pixel of $|X(f)|$ moves, and the readout stays at zero to rounding. Everything the shift does happens in the phase, which acquires a straight ramp of slope $-2\pi t_0$ — that is the sawtooth appearing in the bottom graph, and it gets steeper as you drag further. This is worth being sure about, because it is a common expectation that moving a signal later changes what frequencies are in it. It does not. Where the energy sits in frequency and when it happens in time are carried by two different parts of the same complex number.

$$ x(t - t_0) \;\longleftrightarrow\; X(f)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t_0}, \qquad |X(f) e^{-i 2\pi f t_0}| = |X(f)| $$

The carrier moves the spectrum without changing its shape. Switch it on and the single lobe at zero becomes two lobes at $\pm f_c$, each about half as tall and exactly as wide as before. Nothing about the trade-off changed; the same shape is simply sitting somewhere else. This is what modulation is, and it is the reason the sound on this page needs the carrier: a pulse on its own is centred at $0$ Hz, and $0$ Hz is not a sound.

"About half as tall" is doing some work in that sentence, and the readout will show you how much. The identity below is exact, but it is a sum of two copies, and the copies are not required to keep out of each other's way. Put a rectangle at $4$ ms against a $900$ Hz carrier and the upper lobe comes out $2.6\%$ short of half the area, because the lower copy still has a sidelobe reaching across to $+f_c$. Make the pulse narrower, or the carrier lower, and the shortfall grows; use a Gaussian, whose tails die away faster than any power, and it disappears entirely. The height row shows the measured height and half the area side by side so the difference is visible rather than assumed.

$$ x(t)\cos 2\pi f_c t \;\longleftrightarrow\; \tfrac{1}{2}X(f - f_c) + \tfrac{1}{2}X(f + f_c) $$

What the sound adds. Start it with the carrier on and the width somewhere near $20$ ms: you hear a note, and you could name it. Now drag the width down to $1$ ms. It becomes a click, and the pitch is gone — not quieter, not muffled, gone. The number in the bandwidth row is the same statement: at $1$ ms the energy is spread over roughly a kilohertz, so there is no single frequency there to hear. Your ear needs the signal to last a while before it can say what frequency it is, which is the inequality above with a different measuring instrument. Then leave the width alone and change the shape from rectangle to Gaussian. The rectangle has an audible edge on it, a faint tick at the start and the end; the Gaussian does not, and the only difference between them is how the pulse begins and ends. That is the sidelobes, heard rather than seen — and it is the whole of next week's business with windows.

One honest detail about the sound. It repeats the pulse every —, and a repeating signal does not have the continuous spectrum drawn above: it has a line spectrum, spaced by one over the repeat time, sitting underneath that curve as an envelope. The spacing is under two hertz, far finer than anything on this page is wide, so the shape you see is the shape you hear. The relationship between those two pictures — a Fourier series for something that repeats, a Fourier transform for something that happens once — is the step this week takes.

Where the numbers come from

The solid curve is a numerical integral. The pulse is sampled on a grid, each sample is multiplied by $e^{-i 2\pi f t}$, and the results are added up and scaled by the grid spacing — the midpoint rule, at every frequency across the axis, one sum per frequency. This is deliberately the same computation you would write in a few lines of Python, and it is what the page draws.

The dashed curve is the closed form for the shape you picked, and those are worth having in front of you:

$$ \mathrm{rect} \to T\,\mathrm{sinc}(fT), \qquad \mathrm{triangle} \to \tfrac{T}{2}\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\tfrac{fT}{2}\right) $$ $$ \text{raised cosine} \to \frac{T}{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{sinc}(fT)}{1 - (fT)^2}, \qquad e^{-\pi t^2/T^2} \to T e^{-\pi f^2 T^2} $$

where $\mathrm{sinc}(u) = \sin(\pi u)/(\pi u)$. The triangle one is not a coincidence: a triangle is a rectangle convolved with itself, and convolution in time is multiplication in frequency, so its transform is a sinc squared. That is last week's demo and this week's, in one line.

The two curves are not expected to agree perfectly, and the gap is on the readout. A numerical integral over a finite grid is an approximation, and the row marked "numerical integral against the closed form" is the largest disagreement anywhere on the axis, as a fraction of the peak. It is worth a minute of your attention, because what moves it is not what you would guess.

Leave everything alone and it sits near $10^{-13}$ for a rectangle: the grid runs from one edge of the pulse to the other, so the staircase the computer integrates is the rectangle, exactly. Now drag the shift. It jumps to a few tenths of a percent, because the edges no longer land on grid points and each one is now half in and half out of a sample. Switch on the carrier and it rises again, for a different reason: the thing being integrated now wiggles at the carrier frequency, and the grid has to resolve that too. The smooth shapes behave in the opposite way — a Gaussian with no carrier agrees to about $4 \times 10^{-6}$, better than the rectangle can manage on its best day, because there is no edge anywhere to straddle.

None of that is a defect being confessed. It is the actual behaviour of numerical integration: the error depends on how well a grid can represent the thing you are integrating, not on how complicated the formula looks.

About the numbers on this page. The four closed forms are checked against the defining integral itself, evaluated symbolically, by a set of automated tests; so are the self-duality of the Gaussian, the shift property, and the $\Delta t \cdot \Delta f$ figures. The bandwidths in the table are found from the closed form rather than measured off the picture, so they do not change when you change the zoom.
