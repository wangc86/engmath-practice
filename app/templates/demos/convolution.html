3. Two properties, and what happens without them

Three systems are put through the same two checks. Each check reports how far the system misses by, so a system that passes reports exactly zero.

—
System Superposition: T(a+b) against T(a)+T(b) Time invariance: shift then act, against act then shift
What you just heard

The impulse response is the whole system. Set the input to a single impulse in the first section. The output is $h$ itself, sample for sample — because $x = \delta$ leaves exactly one term in the sum. That is not a special case to memorise, it is the definition of what $h$ is: the answer to "what does this system do to one single sample?". And once you know that, you know what it does to every signal, because every signal is a row of single samples. The click in the second section is the same experiment with your ears: a click one millisecond long against an $h$ hundreds of milliseconds long is near enough to an impulse, so what comes out of the speakers is $h$, played out loud.

An echo is not like convolution; it is convolution. Choose the impulse response with one quieter copy in it, and send the plucked note through. What you hear is the note, then the note again, quieter. Nothing in the code knows the word echo. All it does is add up $x[k]\,h[n-k]$. A room does the same thing: the sound reaches your ear directly, then again off the far wall a few dozen milliseconds later, then again and again, each one quieter. That list of arrival times and sizes is the room's impulse response, and clapping once in a room is how you measure it.

Why the flip. Turn the flip off with a pure delay selected, and look at where the output lands. Sliding $h$ across without reversing it does not delay the signal — it computes something else entirely (it is the cross-correlation, which is a useful operation, but it is not what a system does). The reason for the flip is in the sum itself. The system's answer at time $n$ is built from what the input did before $n$: a sample that arrived at $k$ has by now been ageing for $n-k$ samples, so it contributes $x[k]\,h[n-k]$. Older samples get later parts of $h$. Written as a function of $k$, "later parts of $h$ for smaller $k$" is $h$ read backwards — the flip is bookkeeping for the fact that time runs forwards.

$$ y[n] = \sum_{k} x[k]\,h[n-k] = \sum_{k} h[k]\,x[n-k] $$

Those two sums are the same number, which is worth a moment. It says you may hold either signal still and slide the other one; the answer does not care. Watch the readout for the length of $y$ as you change the two lengths: it is always $N + M - 1$. One sample of input and one sample of response give one sample of output; every extra sample of either one adds one more.

The shape of $h$ and the sound of it. The flat block of $L$ equal taps averages the input over a short window, and the readouts at 200 Hz and 4 kHz tell you what that costs: a slow wave barely notices being averaged over four milliseconds, a fast one is almost wiped out. That is a low-pass filter, and the same block spread over two dimensions is what blurs a photograph. Plus one then minus one does the opposite — its gain for a constant input is exactly zero, so anything slow disappears and only the fast changes survive. In two dimensions that one is an edge detector. Both are convolutions; the only thing that changed is $h$.

Where the convolution sum comes from

The sum is not a definition handed down from nowhere. It is forced by two assumptions, and the third section is where you can watch what happens when either one is dropped.

Superposition. If the system's answer to $a$ is $T(a)$ and its answer to $b$ is $T(b)$, then its answer to $a+b$ must be $T(a) + T(b)$. The clipping system fails this, and it fails it for a reason you can see: each signal on its own stays under the limit, but their sum does not, so the sum gets cut and the two separate answers do not. Nothing about clipping is unreasonable — a loudspeaker driven too hard does exactly that — it simply is not linear, and everything below stops applying to it.

Time invariance. Delaying the input by two samples must delay the output by two samples and change nothing else. The growing gain fails this one while passing the other: it is perfectly linear, but it treats sample number 3 differently from sample number 9, so shifting the input first and shifting the output afterwards give different answers.

Together they leave no freedom. Write the input as a row of single samples, which costs nothing:

$$ x[n] = \sum_{k} x[k]\,\delta[n-k] $$

Time invariance says the answer to $\delta[n-k]$ is $h[n-k]$ — the same response, arriving $k$ samples later. Superposition says the answer to a sum is the sum of the answers, and that a sample scaled by $x[k]$ gives a response scaled by $x[k]$. Put those together:

$$ y[n] = T\!\left(\sum_{k} x[k]\,\delta[n-k]\right) = \sum_{k} x[k]\,T(\delta[n-k]) = \sum_{k} x[k]\,h[n-k] $$

So for a linear time-invariant system there is nothing left to decide. One measurement — the response to a single impulse — settles the answer to every possible input, and the sliding sum is the only way those two properties can be satisfied. It also explains why the third section reports a number rather than a verdict: "linear" and "time-invariant" are not opinions about a system, they are equations it either satisfies or does not, and the residuals are the amount by which it misses.

About the numbers on this page. The sliding sum on this page is written straight from the definition. The sound is computed a second way, by multiplying spectra and transforming back, which is far faster for the tens of thousands of samples involved; a set of automated tests checks the two against each other, and checks both against convolutions worked out symbolically — including the one case you can do by hand, where two rectangular pulses give a trapezium.
